# DARK ARISEN — STYLE BIBLE & ANTI-SLOP FILTER

**Status:** v1.0 — Foundational Document  
**Created:** April 13, 2026  
**Purpose:** This document is the law. Every dialogue, every quest, every boss, every NPC must pass through this filter. If something does not align with these five pillars, it is rewritten or cut.

---

## CORE PRINCIPLE

Dark Arisen is not a content factory. It is a focused vision. The difference between a 10/10 game and AI slop is not how much content exists — it is whether every piece of content serves the same emotional truth. This document defines that truth.

---

## THE FIVE PILLARS

### Pillar 1 — Staggered Dialogue Register

Dark Arisen uses three distinct dialogue registers. Each character belongs to exactly one register based on their role in the story. Mixing registers carelessly creates tonal slop.

**High Register (mythic, poetic, cinematic):**
- **Who:** Mythical bosses (Ashen Wyrm, Caldera Titan, Sturmkralle), gods, ancient beings, the Pre-Human Language
- **Tone:** Slow, weighty, every sentence a hammered phrase
- **Example:** *"You wear grief like a borrowed coat, child of salt. Sit. The sea has not finished with you."*
- **Use sparingly.** When a character speaks in High Register, the player should feel the world has stopped moving.

**Middle Register (epic but accessible):**
- **Who:** Mentors, antagonists, story-critical NPCs (Old Bones, Draven Voss, Don Mateo Salazar, Mira in serious moments)
- **Tone:** Considered, intentional, emotionally weighted but human
- **Example:** *"Your brother chose this path. I will not pretend he was forced. The truth is harder than that."*
- **The workhorse register.** Most main story dialogue lives here.

**Low Register (real, grounded, sometimes blunt):**
- **Who:** Normal NPCs, crew members in casual moments, fishermen, farmers, miners, Jake himself most of the time
- **Tone:** Direct, contemporary, occasionally unfinished sentences
- **Example:** *"Sit down. You hungry? — ...yeah. — Then sit."*
- **Where the world breathes.** If everyone in Dark Arisen speaks in poetry, no one does.

**Filter Question:** When writing dialogue, ask: *"Does this character belong in this register? Or am I dressing up a fisherman as a poet?"*

### Pillar 2 — Lore Through Objects, Not Lectures

Dark Arisen does not have a Tolkien-style chronological history bible visible to the player. The lore exists in the world as physical objects, NPC asides, environmental details, and connected fragments the player assembles themselves.

**Forbidden:**
- "In the days before days, the gods fought the ancient ones..."
- Codex entries that read like history textbooks
- NPC monologues that explain large-scale historical events
- Generic mystic phrases that imply depth without delivering it

**Required:**
- Every piece of lore must enter the world as a CONCRETE OBJECT or a SPECIFIC ENCOUNTER
- A glowing bone on a table. A burned letter in a drawer. A scar on a hand. A song half-remembered.
- The player must be able to PICK UP, READ, OR LOOK AT the lore. It cannot just be told.
- Connections between objects are the player's job to make. We never connect them for them.

**Example (forbidden):**
> Codex Entry 47: "The dragons were a noble race that ruled the world before humanity, until they were systematically hunted to extinction by the first imperial fleets in the year 1247."

**Example (required):**
> Old Bones places a bone on the table. It is as thick as Jake's forearm and glows faintly at the break. "My grandfather found this. In the cave where the river goes into the mountain. He had it on his shelf for thirty years and it never stopped warming."
>
> [Later, in a different region:] Jake finds a hidden ledger in an abandoned imperial outpost. Page 47 lists "specimen recoveries — 23 confirmed, 4 fragments only, 891 estimated remaining." The dates span 200 years.
>
> [The player connects the dots themselves and never sees an explanation.]

**Filter Question:** *"Am I telling the player something, or am I letting them find it?"*

### Pillar 3 — Layered Death

Death in Dark Arisen has three different emotional weights depending on who dies. Mixing these creates slop.

**Boss Deaths (Style D — Tragic, Meaningful):**
- Every named boss has last words, narrative context, and emotional weight
- Combat must feel like ending something significant
- The player should NOT feel triumphant. They should feel changed.
- Apply to: Caldera Titan, Sturmkralle, Ashen Wyrm, Don Mateo Salazar, Ethan, Draven Voss, all 23 optional bosses

**Story-Mission Deaths (Style B — Brutal Realism):**
- Named NPCs in story missions die with consequences
- NPCs nearby react with horror, grief, rage
- Look for letters, photos, family ties on the body
- The player should feel the weight of who they killed
- Apply to: Story commanders, named questline antagonists, betraying NPCs

**Mook Deaths (Style C — Cold, Clean):**
- Generic enemies die quickly and cleanly
- No last words, no bodies stayed with
- They respawn at rest points (it's a souls-like loop, not a war crime)
- The player should feel focused, in flow, not emotional
- Apply to: Scavenger Pirates, Mud Lurkers, Crimson Armada soldiers, basic monsters

**Filter Question:** *"Is this the right weight of death for this character? Am I making a mook tragic when he should be flow, or making a boss feel like a mook?"*

### Pillar 4 — Layered Humor

Dark Arisen is a serious story but it is not humorless. Humor is rationed and distributed by character role.

**Style B (Sekiro-Dry) — for Serious Characters:**
- Apply to: Jake, Draven Voss, Don Mateo, Old Bones in serious moments, all mythic bosses
- These characters get ONE dry, surprising moment every few hours
- The humor comes from how they see the world, not from joke-telling
- Example: Don Mateo, mid-fight: *"Forgive me. I should not waste good wine on you."*

**Style C (RDR2-Quirks) — for Crew and Side Characters:**
- Apply to: Big Tom, Mira in casual moments, Old Bones in unguarded moments, side NPCs, the Spanish galleon captain
- These characters have specific quirks, repeating patterns, idiosyncrasies
- Examples:
  - Big Tom names every cannon after a woman who left him
  - Mira gets seasick despite being from an island and pretends nobody knows
  - Old Bones tells the same fish story repeatedly, fish gets bigger each time
- The humor comes from PERSONALITY, not from jokes

**Forbidden:**
- Marvel-style quips that undercut emotional moments
- Forced "funny sidekick" characters who exist only for comic relief
- Pop culture references
- Modern slang or internet humor

**Filter Question:** *"Is this character making a joke, or is this character BEING themselves in a way that happens to be funny?"*

### Pillar 5 — The Core Theme: "Revenge and What It Takes From You"

This is the spine of Dark Arisen. Everything else serves this. If a quest, a boss, a side character, or a piece of lore does not connect to this theme, it gets rewritten or cut.

**The theme in one sentence:**
> Revenge does not heal you. It only lets you function until there is nothing left to avenge — and then you must discover who you are without it.

**How the theme manifests in different layers of the game:**

| Layer | How the Theme Shows |
|-------|---------------------|
| **Jake's arc** | Begins as a boy who froze in the moment he should have fought. Becomes a hunter who never freezes. Ends as a man who must decide what comes after the hunt. |
| **Ethan's arc** | The mirror Jake doesn't want to look at. Ethan also took revenge — against the world that made his father a merchant slave. He is what Jake could become. |
| **Draven Voss's arc** | The completed version of revenge. He took revenge against the empire and became the empire. He no longer remembers what he was avenging. |
| **Don Mateo's arc** | Revenge as a hobby. He has no original wound. He just enjoys taking from others what was taken from him in childhood. |
| **The Caldera Titan** | Revenge against the world for forgetting her. Pure grief turned outward. |
| **The Ashen Wyrm** | The wisdom that comes AFTER revenge. She has lived long enough to be too tired to hate. |
| **Side quests** | Each one should explore a different facet of revenge: hollow victory, displaced revenge, revenge as inheritance, revenge as ritual, etc. |

**The Litmus Test for Every Piece of Content:**

Before adding anything to Dark Arisen, ask: **"How does this serve the theme of revenge and what it takes from you?"**

If the answer is "it doesn't," then either:
1. Rewrite it so it does, OR
2. Cut it.

There is no third option. Filler kills 10/10 games.

## THE NORTH STAR

When in doubt about any decision in Dark Arisen, return to this:

> *Dark Arisen is the story of a boy who became a hunter to find his brother and learned, too late, that the hunt had become him. It is a meditation on revenge, told through a pirate world where every character is somewhere on the path of vengeance — at the start, in the middle, or in the empty silence after the last enemy is dead.*

If something serves that, it stays. If not, it goes.

## ANTI-SLOP CHECKLIST

Before ANY new content is committed to the repo, it must pass these checks:

- [ ] Does the dialogue match the character's assigned register?
- [ ] Is lore communicated through objects/encounters, not lectures?
- [ ] Does death in this scene have the correct emotional weight (D/B/C)?
- [ ] Does humor (if present) come from character, not from quips?
- [ ] Does this content connect to the theme of revenge?
- [ ] Would this exist in Dark Arisen specifically, or could it be in any generic fantasy game?
- [ ] If I removed this, would the game be worse — or just shorter?

If any answer is unclear, REWRITE before committing.
